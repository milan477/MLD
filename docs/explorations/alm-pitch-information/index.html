Do encoders used in ALMs retain pitch information?

When an audio-language model struggles to identify a note, where does the limitation arise? Is pitch information accessible in the audio encoder, and does it remain accessible after projection into the language model?

I investigated this using linear probes on frozen representations from Audio Flamingo Next (AFN), SALMONN-2, and Qwen2-Audio, measuring pitch-class, octave, and MIDI-pitch decoding across different processing stages.

In this experiment, I evaluated linear probes on 88 held-out notes—one per piano key:

The full dataset contained 440 clips: 420 recorded MAESTRO excerpts and 20 sampled-piano fills. All three models used the same fixed split: 264 training clips (60%), 88 validation clips (20%), and 88 test clips (20%). Each piano key contributed three training clips, one validation clip, and one test clip, with source groups kept disjoint across splits.

Layers were selected using validation data. The final probes were then refitted on the combined training and validation sets (352 clips) and evaluated on the 88 held-out test clips, with matched shuffled-label controls.

These scores measure how well linear probes predict pitch class and octave from selected frozen representations on this dataset. The table alone does not establish whether pitch information is preserved in the encoder’s final output or used by the language model.
